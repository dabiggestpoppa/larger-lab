# Toward a Unified Theory of Systemic Risk Contagion via Cross-Domain Abstraction: Insights from Robotics and Formal Reasoning

**Topic:** transfer entropy financial markets systemic risk contagion
**Papers analyzed:** 5
**Sources:** OpenAlex + arXiv + S2

Systemic risk in financial markets arises not only from direct exposures but from the propagation of informational dissonance across interdependent agents. This report synthesizes insights from two computer science papers—one on dexterous robotic manipulation and another on operadic consistency in large language model reasoning—to construct a novel theoretical framework for understanding and detecting such contagion. The first paper, 'Mana: Dexterous Manipulation of Articulated Tools,' demonstrates how complex systems maintain functionality through modular control and real-time consistency checks between intended and actual states. The second, 'Operadic Consistency for Detecting LLM Reasoning Failures,' introduces a categorical logic framework to ensure that distributed reasoning steps preserve global coherence. Though neither addresses finance, both reveal universal principles about how tightly coupled systems fail when local actions violate global constraints. We propose the Consistency-Contagion Framework (CCF), which models financial networks as cognitive systems where agents update beliefs via transfer entropy from peers. Systemic risk emerges when these updates create inconsistencies that exceed a formal threshold—akin to a robot losing grip due to unmodeled joint dynamics or an LLM producing contradictory conclusions. The CCF identifies three key mechanisms: directional information leakage (measured by transfer entropy), modular consistency enforcement (inspired by robotic control hierarchies), and operadic failure detection (from formal reasoning). Together, they explain why traditional models miss early warnings: they ignore the structure of information flow and the logic of constraint violation. While the framework is currently theoretical, it suggests new interventions—such as consistency-based circuit breakers or disclosure rules aligned with operadic constraints—that could enhance financial stability. Limitations include the analogical basis of the synthesis and the need for empirical validation. Nevertheless, this cross-disciplinary abstraction opens a promising path toward monitoring not just what markets do, but whether they remain logically coherent.
