# Executive Summary

## Geopolitical Dynamics and Emerging Markets: Key Findings, Debates, and Policy Implications

The relationship between geopolitics and emerging markets has emerged as one of the most consequential dynamics shaping the global economic landscape. This synthesis of eight research sources reveals a complex, multi-layered interaction in which geopolitical forces act as both structural constraints and catalysts for transformation across developing economies.

**Key Findings**

The most robust finding across the literature is that geopolitical risk exerts a measurable, negative impact on economic and financial indicators in emerging markets. Research demonstrates that elevated geopolitical risk significantly dampens renewable energy consumption, contracts domestic credit availability, and discourages foreign direct investment flows. These effects are not transient; they embed structural vulnerabilities that compound over time, particularly in economies with weaker institutional frameworks. Energy transitions represent a particularly critical arena of geopolitical contestation, as emerging markets navigate competing pressures from fossil fuel dependencies and clean energy ambitions while being caught between great-power rivalries over critical minerals, technology supply chains, and energy infrastructure.

**Central Debates**

Significant scholarly debate persists on the mediating role of financial development. Some researchers position deeper financial systems as a positive, independent force capable of partially offsetting geopolitical headwinds by channeling capital toward productive investments even amid uncertainty. Others contend that financial development, without accompanying regulatory sophistication, can amplify geopolitical transmission channels, making markets more susceptible to external shocks. This contradiction has profound implications for policy sequencing in developing economies. Additional debates center on whether emerging markets should pursue strategic alignment with major powers or maintain strategic ambiguity to maximize economic opportunities across competing blocs.

**Policy Implications**

For policymakers in emerging markets, the evidence underscores three priorities. First, investing in institutional quality—rule of law, regulatory transparency, and governance capacity—serves as the most effective buffer against geopolitical volatility. Second, financial sector development must be pursued alongside robust macroprudential frameworks to prevent capital flight during geopolitical stress. Third, energy transition strategies should be designed with geopolitical diversification in mind, reducing dependence on any single supplier or technology partner.

For international institutions and developed-market policymakers, the findings suggest that development assistance and trade frameworks must account for geopolitical risk as a first-order variable rather than an exogenous afterthought. Ignoring this dimension risks perpetuating cycles of instability that undermine decades of development progress.

Ultimately, emerging markets are not passive recipients of geopolitical forces but active agents whose institutional choices and strategic positioning will determine whether geopolitical headwinds become tailwinds for resilient, sustainable growth.

### Source 2: The Impact of Geopolitical Risks on Financial Development: Evidence from Emerging Markets

**Analysis of Source 2: The Impact of Geopolitical Risks on Financial Development in Emerging Markets (Lu, Gözgör, Huang, & Lau, 2020)**

**1. Main Argument**
The central argument of this paper is that geopolitical risk (GPR) acts as a significant structural impediment to financial development in emerging markets (EMs). The authors posit that heightened geopolitical tensions—encompassing threats of war, terrorism, and interstate conflict—create an environment of uncertainty that contracts domestic credit allocation to the private sector. This credit contraction, in turn, undermines financial deepening, reduces competitiveness, and ultimately slows economic performance. The paper claims to be the first to empirically establish this specific negative causal linkage for a broad panel of EMs, framing geopolitical stability as a prerequisite for financial sector maturation.

**2. Key Concepts & Frameworks**
*   **Geopolitical Risk (GPR):** Operationalized via the Caldara and Iacoviello (2018) index, capturing both *threats* (acts of terror, war threats) and *acts* (actual wars, conflicts). The framework treats GPR as an exogenous shock to the investment climate.
*   **Financial Development:** Measured narrowly but standardly as **Domestic Credit to Private Sector (% of GDP)**. This serves as a proxy for financial depth and the efficiency of financial intermediation.
*   **Transmission Channels (Implicit):** The paper suggests GPR operates through **uncertainty** and **risk aversion**. Higher GPR increases the perceived probability of capital loss, regulatory arbitrariness, or contract unenforceability, leading banks to tighten lending standards and firms to delay investment.
*   **Control Framework:** Standard growth/finance determinants: *Per Capita Income* (demand for finance), *Broad Money (M2)* (financial depth/liquidity), and *External Imbalances* (current account vulnerability/sudden stop risk).

**3. Methodology**
*   **Design:** Panel data analysis covering **18 emerging markets** over **1985–2018** (34 years).
*   **Primary Estimator:** **Fixed Effects (FE) / Within Estimator**. This controls for unobserved time-invariant country heterogeneity (e.g., legal origin, geography, institutional legacy).
*   **Dynamic Panel Correction:** **Bias-Corrected Least Squares Dummy Variable (LSDVC)** estimator (Kiviet, 1995; Bruno, 2005). This addresses the "Nickell bias" inherent in dynamic panels with a lagged dependent variable (domestic credit persistence) and fixed effects, ensuring consistent estimates even with a moderate time dimension (T=34).
*   **Robustness Protocol:** Extensive sensitivity testing including: (1) Continent-specific exclusion (testing regional drivers); (2) Outlier removal (influence analysis); (3) Additional control variables (omitted variable bias mitigation).

**4. Key Findings**
1.  **Core Result:** Geopolitical risk exerts a **statistically significant and economically meaningful negative effect** on domestic credit to the private sector. This holds across both static FE and dynamic LSDVC specifications.
2.  **Control Variables:**
    *   *Per Capita Income* & *Broad Money (M2)*: Positive and significant drivers of credit growth (consistent with literature).
    *   *External Imbalances*: Negative and significant, confirming that macro-financial vulnerability (current account deficits) crowds out domestic lending.
3.  **Robustness:** The negative GPR coefficient remains stable when excluding any single continent (Asia, Europe, Latin America, Africa/Middle East) and when removing statistical outliers. This rules out the possibility that the result is driven by a specific regional conflict (e.g., Middle East wars) or a few extreme crisis episodes.
4.  **Economic Significance:** The authors emphasize the result is not just statistically robust but "economically robust," implying the magnitude of the coefficient represents a material drag on financial deepening.

**5. Relevance to Research Question ("How does emerging markets x geopolitics work?")**
This source provides a **micro-financial transmission mechanism** answer. It moves the "EM x Geopolitics" debate beyond sovereign spreads or capital flows (the typical macro-finance focus) to the **real economy plumbing: bank lending**. It demonstrates that geopolitics works in EMs by **choking the credit channel**—the primary engine of growth in bank-dominated EM financial systems. It establishes that GPR is not merely a sentiment driver for portfolio flows but a structural determinant of financial sector *supply-side* capacity. This adds a critical "financial development" layer to the research question: geopolitics hinders the *institutional deepening* required for EM convergence.

**6. Strengths & Limitations**
*   **Strengths:**
    *   **Identification Strategy:** Use of LSDVC is a methodological upgrade over standard GMM or simple FE for this panel structure, effectively handling endogeneity from the lagged dependent variable.
    *   **Novelty:** First paper to explicitly link the Caldara/Iacoviello GPR index to *financial development* (credit depth) in EMs, filling a distinct literature gap.
    *   **Robustness Rigor:** The continent-exclusion test is particularly strong for EM studies, where regional clusters often drive results.
*   **Limitations:**
    *   **Narrow Dependent Variable:** "Domestic credit to private sector" is a quantity metric. It ignores *credit quality*, *allocation efficiency*, *interest rate spreads*, or *non-bank finance* (bond/equity markets), which are growing in EMs.
    *   **GPR Index Granularity:** The global GPR index may not capture *bilateral* or *regional* geopolitical risks specific to individual EMs (e.g., Korea vs. Turkey vs. Brazil face vastly different threat vectors). A country-specific GPR measure would strengthen causality.
    *   **Endogeneity/Omitted Variables:** While LSDVC addresses dynamic bias, it does not fully solve reverse causality (weak financial systems → state fragility → conflict) or time-varying omitted variables (e.g., major domestic reforms, commodity price shocks) without instrumental variables.
    *   **Sample Period End (2018):** Misses the COVID-19 geopolitical realignment and the 2022 Russia-Ukraine war structural break.

### Source 6: The Mirage of Exchange Rate Regimes for Emerging Market Countries

# Analysis of Source 6: The Mirage of Exchange Rate Regimes for Emerging Market Countries

## 1. Main Argument

Calvo and Mishkin (2003) argue that the dominant debate over whether emerging market countries should adopt fixed or floating exchange rate regimes is largely a "mirage" — a distraction from the more fundamental determinants of macroeconomic success. The central thesis is that **the choice of exchange rate regime is of second-order importance** compared to the development of sound fiscal, financial, and monetary institutions. Rather than obsessing over the regime choice itself, policymakers in emerging markets should prioritize deep institutional reforms that create resilient economic governance structures. The paper challenges the prevailing orthodoxy that regime selection is a primary lever for macroeconomic stability, reframing it as a symptom rather than a cause of institutional quality.

## 2. Key Concepts & Frameworks

The paper engages with several critical concepts:

- **Exchange Rate Regime Theory**: The standard theoretical framework for choosing between fixed and floating rates, including the Mundell-Fleming model and the "impossible trinity" (trilemma), where countries cannot simultaneously maintain a fixed exchange rate, free capital movement, and independent monetary policy.
- **Institutional Preconditions**: The authors identify institutional traits — such as central bank independence, fiscal discipline, financial sector regulation, and rule of law — that predispose countries toward success under either regime.
- **Endogeneity of Institutions**: The paper explores the bidirectional relationship between regime choice and institutional development, asking whether certain regimes might catalyze or hinder institutional strengthening.
- **Emerging Market Vulnerabilities**: The specific structural weaknesses of emerging economies — including liability dollarization, weak credibility, and shallow financial markets — that make standard regime prescriptions inadequate.

## 3. Methodology

The paper is primarily **theoretical and analytical** rather than empirical. Calvo and Mishkin employ a critical literature review and conceptual analysis, systematically deconstructing the standard theory of exchange rate regime choice and exposing its limitations when applied to emerging market contexts. They draw on institutional economics frameworks and reference historical episodes of emerging market crises to support their arguments. The methodology is characteristic of high-level policy-oriented economic analysis, synthesizing existing theory with institutional realities.

## 4. Key Findings

## 5. Relevance to Research Question

### Source 7: Geopolitical risk, economic policy uncertainty and asset returns in Chinese financial markets

# Analysis of Source 7: Chiang (2021)

## 1. Main Argument

Chiang (2021) argues that geopolitical risk (GPR) and economic policy uncertainty (EPU) exert distinct and asymmetric effects on asset returns and inter-asset correlations within Chinese financial markets. The central thesis is that these two forms of uncertainty—geopolitical risk and domestic policy uncertainty—do not operate uniformly across asset classes. Instead, they produce differentiated responses in stocks, bonds, and gold, with important implications for portfolio diversification and risk management in an emerging market context. The paper positions China as a critical case study for understanding how geopolitical tensions and policy uncertainty interact to shape financial market behavior in a major emerging economy.

## 2. Key Concepts & Frameworks

The paper operates at the intersection of **geopolitical risk**, **economic policy uncertainty**, and **asset pricing** within emerging market finance. Key concepts include:

- **Geopolitical Risk (GPR):** Measured as the absolute value of changes in geopolitical risk (|ΔGPR|), capturing the magnitude of geopolitical shocks regardless of direction.
- **Economic Policy Uncertainty (EPU):** Captured as changes in EPU (ΔEPU), reflecting shifts in domestic policy ambiguity.
- **Dynamic Conditional Correlation (DCC):** Borrowed from Engle (2009), this framework allows for time-varying correlations between asset returns.
- **Rolling Correlation Model:** Chiang's (1988) approach provides a complementary method for tracking evolving inter-asset relationships.
- **Downside Risk:** The paper emphasizes that stock returns are particularly sensitive to downside risk measures, linking uncertainty to asymmetric investor behavior.

The framework implicitly draws on **safe-haven asset theory**, examining whether bonds and gold serve as hedges against different types of uncertainty in China's financial system.

## 3. Methodology

The study employs a **quantitative, time-series econometric approach** using two complementary correlation models:

- **Engle's (2009) DCC Model:** This captures time-varying conditional correlations between asset pairs (stock-bond, stock-gold) in response to changes in EPU and GPR.
- **Chiang's (1988) Rolling Correlation Model:** Provides a robustness check and alternative estimation of dynamic correlations over time.

The dependent variables are asset returns for **stocks, bonds, and gold** in Chinese financial markets. The independent variables are **ΔEPU** (change in economic policy uncertainty) and **|ΔGPR|** (absolute change in geopolitical risk). The use of the absolute value for GPR is notable—it treats increases and decreases in geopolitical risk symmetrically, focusing on the magnitude of geopolitical disruption rather than its direction.

## 4. Key Findings

The paper produces several significant findings:

- **Stock-bond correlations are negatively related to ΔEPU:** When economic policy uncertainty rises, the correlation between stocks and bonds decreases, suggesting that bonds increasingly serve as a hedge against policy-driven uncertainty in China.
- **Stock-gold correlations are positively related to |ΔGPR|:** Higher geopolitical risk increases the co-movement between stocks and gold, suggesting that gold does not function as a clean safe haven during geopolitical crises in China—both assets may move together under geopolitical stress.
- **Stock-gold correlations are negatively related to ΔEPU:** Conversely, when domestic policy uncertainty rises, gold and stocks diverge, suggesting gold may serve as a hedge specifically against policy uncertainty rather than geopolitical risk.
- **Stock returns are adversely affected by downside risk:** Both ΔEPU and |ΔGPR| negatively impact stock returns, with the effect being particularly pronounced for downside risk measures.

These findings reveal a **critical distinction**: geopolitical risk and economic policy uncertainty have fundamentally different effects on asset correlations and returns in Chinese markets.

## 5. Relevance to Research Question

This source is **highly relevant** to the research question on how emerging markets and geopolitics interact. It provides empirical evidence from China—the world's largest emerging market—demonstrating that:

- Geopolitical risk does not affect all asset classes uniformly, challenging simplistic narratives about safe-haven assets in emerging markets.
- The distinction between geopolitical risk and domestic policy uncertainty is analytically crucial; conflating the two leads to misleading conclusions about market behavior.
- Emerging market financial systems respond to geopolitical shocks in ways that differ from developed markets, partly due to institutional structures, capital controls, and the role of state intervention.
- The findings suggest that **geopolitical risk integration into emerging market finance is complex and asset-specific**, with implications for investors, policymakers, and international relations scholars.

## 6. Strengths & Limitations

**Strengths:**
- Rigorous econometric methodology using two complementary models enhances robustness.
- The distinction between GPR and EPU is analytically valuable and often overlooked in the literature.
- Focus on China provides critical evidence from the most significant emerging market.
- The finding that gold behaves differently under geopolitical versus policy uncertainty challenges conventional wisdom.

**Limitations:**
- The study is limited to a single country (China), which may limit generalizability to other emerging markets with different institutional frameworks.
- The use of absolute values for GPR, while methodologically defensible, may obscure important directional effects (e.g., escalating versus de-escalating geopolitical tensions).
- The paper focuses on financial market outcomes and does not deeply explore the **mechanisms** through which geopolitical risk transmits to markets.
- The time period studied may not capture the full range of geopolitical dynamics, particularly more recent escalations in US-China tensions.
- The analysis does not account for potential **endogeneity** between geopolitical events and policy responses.

## 7. Key Quotes/Findings

- "Stock-bond return correlations are negatively correlated to ΔEPU, whereas stock-gold return correlations are positively related to |ΔGPR|."
- "Stock returns are adversely related to the risk/uncertainty measured by downside risk, ΔEPU and |ΔGPR|."
- The core finding that **geopolitical risk and economic policy uncertainty produce divergent effects on asset correlations** represents the paper's most significant contribution to understanding emerging market-geopolitics dynamics.

## 1. Thematic Analysis

The intersection of emerging markets and geopolitics operates across several interconnected thematic domains. Synthesizing the eight sources reveals three dominant themes: **geopolitical risk as a structural constraint on economic development**, **energy transitions as a new arena of geopolitical contestation**, and **institutional quality as the mediating variable** that determines how emerging markets absorb and respond to geopolitical shocks.

### Geopolitical Risk as a Structural Constraint

The most empirically robust theme across the sources concerns the measurable, negative impact of geopolitical risk on key economic and financial indicators in emerging markets. Alsagr and van Hemmen Almazor (2021) demonstrate that geopolitical risk significantly dampens renewable energy consumption, while Lu et al. (2020) establish that it contracts domestic credit to the private sector—the primary engine of growth in bank-dominated emerging market financial systems. Chiang (2021) further shows that geopolitical risk produces asymmetric effects on asset returns in Chinese financial markets, challenging simplistic narratives about safe-haven behavior. Collectively, these findings establish that geopolitical risk is not merely a transient sentiment driver but a **structural determinant** of financial deepening, energy transitions, and investment climates in emerging economies.

### Energy Transitions and Geopolitical Realignment

A second major theme concerns how the global energy transition is reshaping geopolitical power dynamics, with emerging markets occupying a paradoxical position as both potential winners and vulnerable participants. Lebrouhi et al. (2022) and Noussan et al. (2020) argue that hydrogen—particularly green hydrogen—is becoming a new geopolitical instrument. Countries with abundant renewable resources (e.g., Morocco, Chile, Namibia) could become the "new energy superpowers" [Lebrouhi et al., 2022], fundamentally altering their geopolitical standing. However, both sources caution that without equitable technology transfer and international governance, the hydrogen economy risks replicating historical patterns of energy colonialism, where resource-rich emerging markets remain dependent on advanced economies for technology and infrastructure.

### Institutional Quality as Mediating Variable

A third, cross-cutting theme is the decisive role of institutional quality in mediating the relationship between geopolitical forces and economic outcomes. Calvo and Mishkin (2003) argue that institutional development—sound fiscal policy, credible central banking, robust financial regulation—matters far more than technical regime choices in determining macroeconomic stability. Alsagr and van Hemmen Almazor (2021) reinforce this by showing that the negative impact of geopolitical risk on renewable energy adoption is amplified in countries with weaker institutional frameworks. This theme suggests that **geopolitical vulnerability in emerging markets is fundamentally an institutional phenomenon**: states with deeper institutional capacity can absorb and redirect geopolitical shocks, while fragile states experience compounding vulnerabilities.

## 2. Comparative Analysis

### Financial Development vs. Energy Transition

Comparing Sources 1 and 2 reveals a critical tension in how geopolitical risk transmits through emerging market economies. Alsagr and van Hemmen Almazor (2021) focus on the **real economy**—specifically, the adoption of renewable energy technologies—and find that geopolitical risk disrupts policy continuity and diverts fiscal priorities toward security rather than sustainability. Lu et al. (2020), by contrast, focus on the **financial sector plumbing**, demonstrating that geopolitical risk chokes the credit channel by increasing uncertainty and risk aversion among lenders. These complementary findings suggest a **dual transmission mechanism**: geopolitical risk simultaneously undermines the supply of credit (financial channel) and the policy environment for long-term investment (institutional channel), creating a compounded drag on sustainable development.

The comparison also reveals an important asymmetry. Financial development *promotes* renewable energy adoption, while geopolitical risk *deters* it [Alsagr & van Hemmen Almazor, 2021]. Yet geopolitical risk simultaneously undermines the very financial development required to enable the energy transition [Lu et al., 2020]. This creates a **vicious cycle** in which geopolitical instability erodes the institutional and financial preconditions necessary for sustainable development, locking emerging markets into carbon-intensive pathways.

### Hydrogen Geopolitics: Opportunity and Risk

Comparing Sources 3 and 4 on hydrogen reveals a nuanced debate about whether the hydrogen economy will **disrupt or reinforce** existing global power hierarchies. Lebrouhi et al. (2022) emphasize the transformative potential for emerging markets, highlighting how countries like Morocco and Chile could leverage renewable endowments to gain geopolitical influence. Noussan et al. (2020) share this optimism but stress the critical importance of **international governance and transparent standards** to prevent fragmented national strategies from creating new dependencies. Both sources agree that the hydrogen transition carries profound geopolitical implications, but Noussan et al. (2020) are more cautious about the risks of technological dependency, noting that a gap between nations with R&D capacity and those with resource endowments could produce a new form of energy colonialism.

### China as a Distinct Case

Chiang (2021) provides a critical single-country perspective that both complements and complicates the cross-country findings. The discovery that geopolitical risk and economic policy uncertainty produce **divergent effects** on asset correlations in Chinese markets—gold serves as a hedge against policy uncertainty but not against geopolitical risk—suggests that the relationship between geopolitics and financial markets is context-dependent and asset-specific. This finding challenges the generalizability of cross-country panel results and underscores the importance of domestic institutional structures, capital account management, and state intervention in mediating geopolitical transmission.

## 3. Theoretical Frameworks

## 4. Methodological Comparison

The sources employ a diverse range of methodologies, each with distinct strengths and limitations for addressing the research question.

Chiang (2021) uses Dynamic Conditional Correlation (DCC) and Rolling Correlation models to capture time-varying inter-asset relationships in Chinese markets. These methods offer statistical rigor and generalizability but rely on aggregate national data that may obscure subnational variation and assume that indices like the GPR uniformly capture relevant risks across diverse contexts.

**Qualitative Review and Policy Analysis** characterizes Sources 3, 4, and 8. Lebrouhi et al. (2022) and Noussan et al. (2020) synthesize literature, policy documents, and industry reports to map the geopolitical dimensions of hydrogen development. Source 8 employs multidisciplinary synthesis combining climate models, observational data, and scenario-based projections. These approaches provide rich contextual insight but lack the empirical falsifiability of quantitative methods.

**Theoretical-Analytical** methodology is used by Calvo and Mishkin (2003), who employ critical literature review and conceptual analysis to challenge prevailing orthodoxies about exchange rate regimes. This approach is powerful for reframing debates but does not provide systematic empirical testing.

**Expert Synthesis** is the method in Source 5, which triangulates perspectives from 50+ scholars across disciplines. While valuable for mapping an emerging field like the metaverse, this approach lacks methodological transparency and primary data.

The methodological diversity across sources is both a strength and a limitation. It enables triangulation across different approaches but makes direct comparison and synthesis challenging, particularly when quantitative studies use different estimators, time periods, and operationalizations of key variables.

Calvo and Mishkin's (2003) argument about the **primacy of institutional quality over exchange rate regime choice** remains theoretically influential but is dated and lacks contemporary empirical validation, particularly in light of post-2008 developments including China's managed exchange rate and the growing role of geopolitical sanctions.

Source 8's analysis of **climate change as a threat multiplier** in the Mediterranean is well-supported by climate models and observational data but treats the region as relatively monolithic, obscuring important cross-country variation in adaptive capacity and institutional resilience.

The synthesis yields several emergent insights that transcend individual sources:

**Fifth, climate change functions as a geopolitical threat multiplier** that interacts with and amplifies existing vulnerabilities in emerging markets [Source 8]. Future research must move beyond siloed analyses to develop integrated frameworks that capture the compound, interconnected nature of geopolitical risk in an era of energy transition, climate change, and digital transformation.

## 4. Contradictions and Debates

# Contradiction Analysis: Emerging Markets × Geopolitics

## 1. Direct Contradictions

The most salient direct contradiction concerns the **role of financial development** in mediating the relationship between geopolitical risk and emerging market outcomes. Source 1 (Alsagr & van Hemmen Almazor, 2021) positions financial development as a *positive, independent force* that promotes renewable energy consumption—implying that deeper financial systems can partially offset geopolitical headwinds. In contrast, Source 2 (Lu et al., 2020) argues that geopolitical risk *directly undermines* financial development itself by contracting domestic credit to the private sector. This creates a logical tension: if GPR degrades financial development (Source 2), and financial development enables positive outcomes like renewable energy adoption (Source 1), then the two sources together suggest a **vicious cycle** rather than independent effects. Neither paper acknowledges this recursive relationship, creating an apparent contradiction in whether financial development is a resilient buffer or a vulnerable transmission channel.

A second contradiction emerges around **geographic agency and power dynamics**. Sources 3 and 4 (Lebrouhi et al., 2022; Noussan et al., 2020) present emerging markets as potentially empowered actors in the hydrogen transition—nations like Morocco, Chile, and Namibia could become "new energy superpowers" through renewable resource endowments. Yet Source 8 (Mediterranean climate study) portrays emerging economies in a structurally subordinate position, facing "disproportionate climate risks due to limited adaptive capacity and institutional fragility." The former framing emphasizes agency and opportunity; the latter emphasizes vulnerability and dependency. These are not strictly incompatible, but they reflect fundamentally different assumptions about whether emerging markets are *shapers* or *subjects* of geopolitical change.

A third tension exists between Source 6 (Calvo & Mishkin, 2003), which argues that exchange rate regime choice is a "mirage"—second-order to institutional quality—and the implicit argument in Sources 1, 2, and 7 that specific policy and financial variables (credit markets, asset correlations, energy investment) are first-order determinants of outcomes. If institutions are truly paramount, then the granular financial mechanisms identified in other sources may be epiphenomenal.

## 2. Methodological Conflicts

The sources exhibit a sharp **quantitative-qualitative divide** that generates conflicting epistemological assumptions. Sources 1, 2, and 7 employ rigorous panel econometrics (GMM, LSDVC, DCC models) with explicit causal identification strategies, treating geopolitical risk as a measurable, exogenous shock. Sources 3, 4, 5, and 8 rely on qualitative synthesis, literature review, and expert opinion, treating geopolitics as an interpretive, structural condition. These methodological choices are not merely stylistic—they produce different *kinds* of knowledge. The quantitative studies find specific, bounded effects (e.g., "GPR reduces domestic credit by X%"), while the qualitative studies make sweeping structural claims (e.g., "hydrogen is a new geopolitical instrument") that resist falsification.

A further conflict arises in **temporal framing**. Source 2 uses data from 1985–2018, capturing the post-Cold War liberalization era, while Source 7 focuses on Chinese financial markets during a period of rising US-China tensions. Source 6, published in 2003, predates the global financial crisis, the rise of geopolitical risk indices, and the current era of great-power competition. The findings may reflect period-specific dynamics rather than universal relationships, yet none of the sources adequately addresses temporal heterogeneity.

Additionally, the **level of analysis** varies dramatically: Source 7 examines intra-market asset correlations (micro-financial), Source 2 studies national credit markets (macro-financial), Source 3 analyzes global hydrogen geopolitics (systemic), and Source 5 speculates about metaverse governance (emergent/forward-looking). These different scales of analysis are not easily commensurable, creating apparent contradictions that may simply reflect different units of observation.

## 3. Contextual Differences

The sources span vastly different **thematic domains**—energy transition (Sources 1, 3, 4), financial development (Sources 2, 6, 7), digital infrastructure (Source 5), and climate-security nexus (Source 8)—each with distinct causal logics. Geopolitical risk may operate differently in energy markets (where it disrupts long-term investment horizons) than in financial markets (where it triggers immediate capital flight) or in climate systems (where it compounds slow-onset environmental degradation). The apparent contradictions may therefore reflect genuine domain-specific dynamics rather than analytical errors.

**Regional variation** also matters. Source 8 focuses on the Mediterranean, where climate intersects with migration and EU-North Africa power asymmetries. Source 7 centers on China, a unique emerging market with state-controlled capital markets and geopolitical great-power status. Source 2 covers 18 diverse emerging markets across continents. The generalizability of findings across these contexts is uncertain, and contradictions may reflect the absence of a unified "emerging market" category.

## 4. Severity Assessment
